# What IF You Owned a Real Iron-Man Suit?

You tap your chest. The suit snaps on. Arms, leggings, helmet. 1 second. A car just lost its brakes. It's rolling down the hill straight at the crowd at the street fair. You land in front of it. One hand, the car stops 2 ft from a little kid holding an ice cream. The whole street goes quiet. Who are you? That's you 3 months from now. Because yesterday a metal case showed up on your doorstep. Your name on it. No return address. Inside, a real Iron Man suit. And it only works for you. Learn it all and you could lift a truck, outrun a sports car, fly faster than sound. And that's when everyone starts asking who's inside. But right now, it's day one. You put it on in your garage and you can't even lift your arm. Your best friend tosses you a soda. You try to catch it. The glove crushes it. Soda everywhere. Dude, are you okay in there? See, the suit is like a robot body you wear. It weighs about 400 lb. Motors carry the weight so you don't feel it. But every part only does what your body tells it to do. Move your hand a little too fast and the glove moves way too fast. A normal person is the boss of their own body. You have to learn a second one, one part at a time. And there's one more rule. The battery lasts about an hour of flying. After that, the suit is a 400-lb statue with you inside. Day one, the problem isn't the suit. It's you. You try to walk to the kitchen. Step one, fine. Step two, you go straight through the wall. So you start with the easiest part, the helmet. The screen inside lights up. It sees in the dark. It sees heat through walls. It zooms 50 times closer than your eyes. At the batting cage, the machine throws a ball at 90 mph. A normal batter gets less than half a second to swing. The helmet draws a line where the ball is going. You hit 20 in a row. Your friend can't stop laughing. "That's not fair." It's not. At night, you play hide and seek with the whole block. They hide in the dark. You see their body heat glowing through the bushes. You find all nine in 2 minutes. Week one, the gloves. Real robot suits already exist. Some let a worker lift 200 lb like it's 20. That's 10 times stronger. Yours is stronger than that. A normal person your age can lift maybe 100 lb. With the gloves, you can lift 50 times that. But first, you break everything. Doorknobs, your phone, three phones, your mom's favorite mug. You practice on eggs. Egg one, crushed. Egg 40, crushed. Egg 91, you pick it up without a crack. Then, you arm wrestle the biggest guy in school. You don't even look at him. You just yawn and win. Your neighbor's pickup truck gets stuck in the mud. 5,000 lb. You lift the back end. He drops his coffee. Week two, the boots. The fastest human ever, Usain Bolt, hit about 27 mph. In the boots, you hit 40 on your first try. You race the school bus. You win. Your whole class watches through the windows. You jump onto your roof, straight up, 20 ft. Then, you miss the landing and fall through the porch. Week three, the hand thrusters. You try to hover 1 ft off the ground. You fly into the garage door, then the fence, then the pool. Try 32, you float. Hands out, perfectly still. Your friend is filming. "Do something cool." You flip, land, fist on the ground. 2 million views by Monday. That night, you hover outside your crush's window. You knock on the glass. She screams. Then, she laughs. Then, she asks if you can take her up, too. You can't. Not yet. Week four, the armor. Your friends want to test it, so they bring paintball guns, 20 of them. You stand in the middle of the field, arms crossed. 200 hits, not one gets through. Then, they try a hockey puck, slap shot, about 100 miles an hour. It bounces off your chest, you don't even blink. Then, it's your turn. Water balloons dropped from 40 ft up. They never see it coming. Month two, full flight. Real jet suits already exist, too. The fastest one hit about 85 miles an hour for a few minutes before the fuel ran out. You hit 300. Then, you fly over the big game. Halftime, 70,000 fans. You do one lap around the stadium, and the whole place starts chanting. The quarterback looks up, points at you. You throw him the game ball from 50 ft up. Perfect spiral. He catches it. 70,000 people scream your name. Well, the suit's name. But, where it gets crazy is what all that speed does to your body. You pull a sharp turn at full speed. The force pushes all the blood out of your head. Most people black out at about five times their own weight. Fighter pilots train for years to take nine. Everything goes gray, then black. You wake up in a cornfield. The suit landed itself. So, you learn to turn slower and to breathe the way pilots do. Squeeze, breathe, squeeze. Then, you fly into a storm, and lightning hits you. A bolt of lightning is hotter than the surface of the sun, but the electricity flows around the metal shell, not through you. It's the same reason airplanes get hit by lightning about once a year and keep flying. Your screen flickers, then comes back on. You just keep going. Month three, faster than sound. That's about 767 mph. When you break it, the air explodes behind you, a sonic boom. Windows shake for miles. Car alarms go off across the whole city. A pilot 5 miles away reports a mystery object on his radar. It's you, doing loops. The next morning, the news is all about you. Then, you start using it for real. A hiker goes missing in the mountains. It's night. It's freezing. Search teams can't see a thing. You fly over the forest and switch to heat vision. One tiny glowing spot. You land next to him. He thinks he's dreaming. Doctors say one more night out there could have killed him. A building is on fire. 30 floors up, a family is stuck on a balcony. Fire truck ladders only reach about 10 floors. You don't need a ladder. Three trips, everybody out. A real firefighter's suit can handle about 500° for a few minutes. Your suit walks straight through the flames. And then, the car at the street fair, the one rolling down the hill. One hand. Who are you? You just fly away, because that's what heroes do. A getaway car speeds away from a bank, police chasing. You drop in front of it, grab the bumper, lift. The wheels spin in the air. The driver just puts his hands up. The world loses its mind. Your face is on every channel. Kids everywhere wear cardboard helmets to school. A late-night show calls you the kid in the can. The army wants the suit, scientists want the suit. Somebody tries to steal it from your garage at 3:00 in the morning. So, you start sleeping next to it. Then, the fakes show up. People online selling copies of your suit. Some of them catch fire. And there's a cost. Your ears ring all the time. The thrusters are as loud as a jet taking off, even with the helmet on. Your neck hurts every morning. The suit can take the hits, your body still feels them. And people stop seeing you. They only see the suit. Your friends don't ask you to hang out anymore. They ask if they can try it on. You haven't been picked for a pickup game in months. Your best friend just shrugs. It's not fair if you play. He's right. Some nights you take the suit off and you feel smaller than before you ever put it on. Six months later, a storm. The big bridge downtown. A cable snaps and a school bus slides off the road. It's hanging over the edge, 200 ft above the river. 30 kids inside. You get there in 9 seconds. The wind is pushing the bus. Every few seconds it slides another inch. The screen in your helmet flashes red, power 4%. You grab the bus. It's too heavy to lift and fly at the same time. Not with 4%. The suit gives you a choice. All power to the arms. And the thrusters shut off. Or, keep flying and let go. If you lift the bus, you can't stay in the air. You'll fall 200 ft into the river in a 400 lb suit. Nobody else in the world can make this choice. Nobody else is wearing the suit. A kid is pressing her hands on the back window, looking right at you. You could call for help. There's no time. The cable groans. The bus slips another inch. Your hands are shaking. Not the suit's, yours. You say it out loud. All power to the arms. The thrusters go quiet. You push. Your arms are screaming. The bus scrapes back over the edge onto the road. All four wheels. 30 kids safe. Then the bridge pulls away above you and you're falling. You hit the water. Everything goes dark. The suit pulls you down like an anchor. The screen flickers. Eject. The suit opens like a shell. You swim up. Your lungs are burning and you break the surface. Behind you, the suit sinks to the bottom of the river. The rescue diver pulls you onto the boat. 20 years on the job, never seen anyone let go of a flying suit to hold up a bus. The kid from the back window runs up and hugs you. She doesn't care that you're not wearing it. You came back for us. For the first time in months, people aren't looking at the suit. They're looking at you. Your best friend pushes through the crowd. He's soaked from the rain. He doesn't ask about the suit. Pick up game tomorrow? Yeah, I'm in. That night, there's another case on your doorstep. Bigger this time. Same handwriting. Inside is a new suit and a note. Test one, passed. There are six more of you. Time to meet them.
