# What If Your Mind Aged 10x Faster Than Your Body?

You've been sitting in maths for what feels like an hour. The clock says 6 minutes. You've already done the worksheet in your head, twice. You've planned your weekend. You've replayed an argument from last week and won [clears throat] it this time. The teacher turns around, very slowly. Good morning. At break, a football comes flying at your head. Except it doesn't fly, it drifts. You watch it spin. You read the logo. You have time to wonder who kicked it. Then it hits you in the face. Because your head still moves at normal speed. You all right? Yeah, I saw it coming for ages. A normal brain keeps time with the world. A second feels like a second. A lesson feels like a lesson. And over a whole lifetime, your mind and your body grow old together, side by side, same pace. Not you. Your body is 16. Your mind is sprinting ahead, 10 days for every one. So what if your mind aged 10 times faster than your body? Here's how it works. Your brain has about 86 billion nerve cells. They talk to each other with tiny electrical pulses. Every thought, every memory, every feeling is billions of those pulses firing in patterns. And your sense of time? It isn't a clock. There's no ticking in your head. Your brain judges time by how much it notices. More moments noticed, more time felt. Your brain does all of it 10 times faster. Every pulse, every pattern, every thought. So every real second, you get 10. Every hour, 10. Every day, your mind lives 10 days. And every year, your mind gets 10 years older. But, here's the rule. Only your mind speeds up. Your body doesn't. Your eyes, your nerves, your muscles, all normal speed. You can think in slow motion. You can't move in it. So, you're 16, and by the time your body is 21, your mind will be 66. And at first, it's brilliant. A 1-hour lesson is 10 hours of thinking time. You finish your homework in your head before the register. A 2-hour exam? 20 hours. You plan every answer. Then, you check it. Then, you check it again. Your hand still writes at normal speed, but it never writes the wrong thing. Someone says something rude. You get a full minute to come up with the perfect comeback, and you deliver it instantly. Online bullet chess gives you 1 minute for the whole game. For you, that's 10. You climb the rankings in a week. Family quiz night? You buzz in first every time. Then, you sit there waiting for them to finish reading the question. 6 weeks of summer holiday? For you, that's over a year. You learn the guitar. You learn to juggle. You read every book in the house, including the manual for the boiler. You pass your driving test on your 17th birthday. Zero mistakes. The examiner says she's never seen anyone check their mirrors so calmly. And Gran? Gran says you've always been an old soul. You're starting to think she's right. But, where it gets really crazy is how you see the world. Films. A film is just 24 still pictures a second. Your brain blends them together into motion. Yours can't keep up the trick. To you, it's about 2 and 1/2 pictures a second. Every action film is a flick book. The cinema gives you a refund. And it gets crazier. Lightning. One flash is often three or four separate strikes down the same path a few hundredths of a second apart. Normal people see a flicker. You see every single strike. One, two, three, four. Like a drumbeat. And it gets crazier. Flies. A housefly takes in the world about four times faster than you do. That's why you can never swat one. It watches your hand coming in slow motion. But now, you're faster than the fly. Your hand isn't. Your brain is. You watch it pick a spot to land. You get there first. You catch it in a cup. First try. Football. A hard penalty reaches the goal in about half a second. Goalkeepers have to guess. You don't. To you, the run-up lasts for ages. You read the hips, the planted foot, the eyes. You know where it's going before it's kicked. You dive at normal speed, but you dive early. The school team makes you keeper. You let in two goals all season. Blinking. You blink about 15 times a minute. Each blink can last up to a third of a second. A normal brain hides that from you. Yours can't hide it fast enough. For you, a blink is up to 3 seconds of darkness 15 times a minute. So, you learn to blink between sentences. And it gets crazier. Talking. Most people speak at about two and a half words a second. To you, that's one word every 4 seconds. Hello. So, you learn to read faces. You know how a sentence ends before it does. You finish everyone's sentences in your head, politely. And it gets even crazier. Dreams. Every night, a normal sleeper spends about 2 hours dreaming. For you, that's 20. You have dreams that last a whole day. You wake up and for a moment, you're not sure what year it is. Of course, the world wants to know about you. The scientists go first. A light flashes. You press a button. Most people take about a quarter of a second. You're quicker, but nowhere near 10 times quicker. Because your finger isn't. So, they try something else. Scientists once dropped volunteers about 30 m into a safety net with a number flashing on a screen on their wrist. Too fast to read. The fall felt slower, but nobody could read the number. Time didn't really slow down. It just felt like it. They drop you. You read every number. You read them out loud on the way down. And you ask to go again. Then, they scan your brain. It's burning fuel like a sprinter's legs. A normal brain uses about a fifth of all your body's energy. Yours wants much more. You eat five meals a day and you're always hungry. Then, the fame. A video of you catching a fly with chopsticks gets 40 million views. The internet calls you slow-mo. A TV quiz show invites you on. You win every round. So, they have your time. You still win. They don't invite you back. A pro gaming team offers you a contract. Then, the league bans you. Unfair advantage. And the chess website sends a polite email. You're banned from bullet chess for life. And the rules get strange. Your body is 20, your mind is 56. So, how old are you? The law says your birthday. You apply for a senior rail card anyway. It's for over 60s. They check your date of birth. No. And there's a cost. Your best mate is 17. Still funny. Still telling the same joke about the PE teacher. You still laugh. But to you, it takes them 3 minutes to get to the punchline. And your mind is 26, then 36. You love them. You're just very far ahead. Your mum is 46. On your 19th birthday, your mind turns 46, too. That morning, you give her advice about her job. Good advice. She takes it. Then she goes quiet. When did you grow up, love? You don't have an answer. It happened while she was blinking. And everything is waiting. A kettle takes about 3 minutes to boil. For you, half an hour. Every queue, every bus, every slow loading screen, 10 times longer. Every day is 10 days long, and nobody else is in them with you. And your brain is getting older. Not just wiser, older. Thinking speed peaks at about 18, then slowly drifts down. Names slip. Words sit on the tip of your tongue. You walk into a room and forget why. That's normal aging. It's just happening to you at 20. And you stop saying I can't wait, because you always have to. 5 years later. You're 21. Your mind is 66. Gran is 74. On the inside, you're catching her up. Your brain isn't as quick as it was. Older brains slow down, but it's still 10 times faster than anyone else's, and it knows so much more. Your vocabulary is the biggest it will ever be. You do Grand's crossword in pen, if she lets you, mostly. Sunday, you're driving Grand home, the motorway, 70 miles an hour. She's telling you, again, that you've always been an old soul. Then the lorry ahead swerves. Its trailer swings out across all three lanes, jackknifed, a wall of metal. At 70, it takes about 96 m to stop. The lorry is 60 m away. You are not going to stop in time. A normal driver gets about 2/3 of a second to react, enough time for one thing, slam the brakes, and hope. You get 7 seconds. Everything goes quiet. Grand's mouth is open, mid-word. A crisp packet hangs in the air. Straight on, you hit the trailer at about 40, both of you, head-on. Left, the hard shoulder. There's a gap, just, but there's a man standing in it, by his broken-down van. Right, the central barrier, steel. Steer into it, and it scrapes your speed away, but your side of the car takes the hit. Grand's side doesn't. Seven whole seconds. That's a long time to look at a choice like that. Then you choose. Brake, steer right. Your hands move so slowly, like they're moving through honey. Metal on metal, sparks across your window. Your door folds. Then, stop. Silence. Dust from the airbag, floating in the light. A hiss from the engine. Then, Gran. Well, that was exciting. She's fine. Not a scratch. Her crossword is still on her lap. You? A broken arm, two cracked ribs. Your door is somewhere in the central reservation, and the trailer stopped 1 m from your bonnet. A paramedic crouches by the window, looks at the barrier, the skid marks, the trailer, then looks at you for a long time. 20 years I've done this job. Everybody breaks. Nobody steers into the barrier. Nobody's got time to. I had time. Gran squeezes your good hand. Old soul. A week later, a knock at the door. A stranger, a long gray coat, an hourglass in one hand. Your mind is 66. I can make it 21 again. Same as your body, normal speed, for good. What's the catch? You forget. Everything since you were 16. 45 years of it. You look at Gran's crossword, still on the table. The stranger turns the hourglass over, and the sand just hangs there. So, would you trade 45 years of memories for a young mind again?
