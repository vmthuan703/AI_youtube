# What IF You Could See Everyone's Stats Like a Video-Game?
video_id: E-e5s70rhHo | đăng: 2026-11-07 | lượt xem: 9108 | outlier: 0.09

You look at his fortune. The power, 88, is immense. Stamina, nine. The entire dining room is watching. The biggest guy in school is holding your hand. There are 20 dollars on the table. Everyone took out their phones. Ready? Let's start! He presses. You do n't resist. You're barely holding on. 5 seconds, 10. His stamina bar is dropping. Yellow, red. His hand starts to tremble. And now you're pushing. Bang! Silence fell in the dining room. And then an explosion of emotions. Your best friend grabs your shoulders. How did you do it? Since the very morning, a small window has appeared above the head of everyone on Earth. Name, level, health bar and characteristics . Just like in a video game. No one else sees them but you. Your friend's window shows level 15. The teacher is level 44. The guy you just defeated has a normal health bar. But the pride scale—no. And next year you'll be reading the fortunes of pros, entire stadiums, and strangers. You will see things that even their doctors don't notice. And that's when it stops being fun. But now you just won $20 and you can't feel your hand. The big guy is still staring at his hand. Revenge? No. You see, the average person guesses. Big muscles—probably strong. Tall— probably fast. And they are wrong all the time. You don't guess, you read. Each characteristic is from 1 to 100. 50 is a normal adult. Strength, speed , endurance, skill, luck. The level is simply their age. Your grandmother is level 82. Scouts spend years learning to recognize talent. And yet they are most often wrong. You understand everything at a glance. The health scale is green when a person is fine, yellow when injured or sick, and red when in real trouble. And everything is updated in real time. When someone gets tired, stamina drops. When someone is nervous, a small nervousness scale fills up. There is only one nuance. You see everyone's wealth except your own. At first you use it to show off. Street basketball. Two captains. Everyone chooses tall ones. You choose that short guy in the corner. Accuracy, 94. You win 21:4. Who is this guy? In physical education, you test the teacher. Patience, four. So for the first time in your life, you're not talking in PE. There is a dynamometer at the school fair . A crowd of graduates takes turns trying their hand. You read every one of them. Then you bet the loudest guy five bucks that the quiet kid with the glasses will beat his record. Strength, 79. The quiet guy hits the top lantern. The whole crowd is going crazy. In knockouts, you check everyone's reaction rate and only knock out the slow ones. Bach. Bach. Bach. The last one standing in every game. At poker night, everyone bets on snacks. You don't look at the cards. You're looking at the nerve scale. Your friend says he has a great combination. His nerves are at 90. He's bluffing. You go home with every bag of chips in the house. Your cousin bets you 20 bucks on a video game tournament. You choose the player with the highest reaction rate. The average person reacts in about a quarter of a second. Your boyfriend is closer to 1/7. He wins, you win. Again. At the end of the first month, you won $300, two pizzas, a skateboard, and someone's lunch for the whole week. And then one day you look at your best friend . You're really looking. He is thin. He gets caught in his own shoelaces. He has never won anything in his life. Boxing, 97. And next to it—a tiny golden word. Potential . I? Boxing? I've never even hit a pillow . You're still dragging him to the box. You are the first to read each sparring partner : who is tired, who is lowering their left hand, who is scared. His stamina is at 30. Keep moving. He will surrender in the second round. He surrenders in the second round. Amateur rounds last about 3 minutes. That's a long time to be tired. So in each round you keep an eye on the other guy's stamina and tell your friend exactly when to stop running and start hitting. After 6 months, your best friend won 11 fights in a row. His boxing performance is also growing. 40, 60, 75. You watch him rise a little after each fight. Then you go to the football stadium. 60,000 people. And you read every one of them. The fastest player on the field runs about 35 km/ h, but in the 40th row, the guy eating the hot dog has a speed rating of 99. He's faster than every player down there. He delivers pizza. You tell the coach. Nobody believes you. To view. After 2 weeks, the pizza guy signs a professional contract. And the rumors are spreading. They're starting to call you "the statistics guy." School coaches call, then college teams, then professional teams. Someone offers you a million dollars a year to sit in the stands and point fingers. Of the half a million basketball players in America's high schools, only about 60 make it into the draft each year. You can find each of them by simply looking . The shoe company wants to see your face on the sneakers. Your school hangs a banner in the gym. The famous coach doesn't believe it. So he puts 100 players on the field and gives you 2 minutes. You name the top 10. In order. He checks their results from last season. You guessed all ten. He just stares at you. Boy, who are you? Scientists want to scan your brain. The TV show wants you to read the statistics of the entire live audience. You voice one man's level of luck on air. It is equal to one. Then it gets caught up in the cable. But the craziest thing is what else these numbers can see. A man walks past you in the mall, laughing. Looks quite healthy. His health bar is flashing red, not yellow, but red, flashing. This shade of red you've only seen in people in hospital beds. About 800,000 Americans have a heart attack each year , and some of them barely feel it coming. You call 911. He says you're crazy. 10 minutes later he's on the floor, and the ambulance is already arriving. In a pool, a child in deep water looks like he's playing. She doesn't squirm, she doesn't scream. Her stamina is at two. True drowning is silent. A drowning person usually cannot even call for help. Many go to the bottom in less than a minute. You dive before the rescuer has time to look back. You pull it to the curb. He coughs, inhales, and his stamina bar begins to fill—green. Soon the hospitals start calling. They're not interested in sports, they just want you to walk down the halls and point fingers . But not everyone wants to use you for good. A man in a shiny jacket offers you $10,000 an evening to sit in on underground fights and tell him who loses. You say "no." He still hands you his business card. At first everyone says "no." And you have to pay for it . You can't turn it off, ever. The average brain can only hold about four things at a time. At a concert, you see 20,000 windows with statistics. Every evening ends with a headache. You start seeing numbers instead of faces. You catch yourself choosing friends based on their characteristics. The short guy on the basketball team asks why you stopped picking him. You tell him the truth. His defense is 12. The next day he leaves the team. The girl you like sits next to you in class. Every time you talk to her, her mood bar drops a little. At dinner, mom says she's fine. But her stress scale reads 95 every night . And every time you visit Grandma, her health bar gets shorter. No one else knows, and you can't dare tell her. Some nights you look in the mirror. There is nothing above your head . No frame, no numbers, just you. And you wonder what would be written in your characteristics. A year later, a packed arena, 10,000 people, national finals. Your best friend is in the red corner, and you are in his corner with a towel. He wins . Round one, round two. Every blow he strikes, you have predicted in advance. His left hand drops after the jab. Now! Babah! Another guy flies into the ropes. The crowd screams so loud you can feel it in your chest. Then, in the last round, he takes one hard blow to the head. He shakes off, smiles, and continues the fight. But his health bar is flashing red. Not from fatigue. Something is wrong inside. A hard blow to the head can rupture a tiny blood vessel near the brain. At first, a person may feel completely normal. If you get to the surgery within 4 hours, most people survive. If you wait too long—most don't. 40 seconds left . He keeps track of all the judges' cards. Hold on, and he's a champion. This is what he trained for every day. If you throw in the towel, he loses. Right here , in front of everyone. He may never get that chance again and may never forgive you. He looks at you, smiling. "I'll handle it." The referee doesn't see anything. The doctor at the ring sees nothing. 10,000 people see the winner. Only you see the color red. You throw in the towel. The arena whistles loudly. Your best friend turns around. He can't believe it. "What are you doing?" At the hospital, a scan shows a small hemorrhage exactly where the blow came. The doctor says, "One more blow and he might not wake up." Since they noticed in time, everything will be fine with him. He is operated on that same night. By morning, his health bar had changed from red to yellow, and then to green. He doesn't say anything for a long time. Then: "You saw this, right?" "Yes. And next year we will win." The next morning, a repeat is shown on every channel. Not a punch, but a towel. And for the first time in a year, people stop calling you "the guy with the stats." They just call you their best friend. A week later, a woman in a gray suit is waiting for you near the gym. You look up to read her characteristics. Nothing. No name, no level, just three question marks. "You see them, right?" "Good, because I can see yours."
