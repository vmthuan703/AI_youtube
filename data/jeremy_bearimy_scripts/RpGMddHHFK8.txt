# What if Your Reaction Time was 10x Slower?

Someone throws you a set of keys. You watch them fly. You watch them hit you in the chest. You watch them land on the floor. Then, your hands come up catching nothing. A balloon pops right next to your ear. Everyone in the room jumps. You don't. You just keep talking. Your mate stares at you. Are you all right? You are. Your body just hasn't found out yet. A normal human is fast. See something and your body moves in about a quarter of a second. It's how people catch a falling phone, dodge a splash, slam on the brakes. Not you. Everything you see, you see right now. Everything you think, you think right now. But your body? Your body's running late. So what if your reaction time was 10 times slower? Here's how it works. Every reaction has three steps. See, think, move. Your eyes send a signal to your brain. Your brain decides what to do. Then it fires a signal down to your muscles. In a normal person, that whole trip takes about a quarter of a second. Yours takes two and a half. Your eyes are fine. Your brain is fine. It's the go signal that waits. It sits there and sits there and then it goes. But here's the rule. Once you've decided, you can't take it back. Whatever you choose, your body does it two and a half seconds later, no matter what's changed. Every move you make is a promise to the future. And honestly, some of it's brilliant. Jump scares don't work on you. The whole cinema screams. You keep eating your popcorn. The nurse says sharp scratch. You don't even flinch. She checks you're still breathing. Your little brother tries to tickle you. Nothing. Nothing. Then you burst out laughing long after he's given up. You win every staring contest. Your blinks run late, too. A spider drops onto your shoulder. You've got loads of time to think about it. Oh, spider. The sea in January? You're in up to your neck before your body's even noticed. And then granddad in his flat cap teaches you the trick. Don't watch where it is, watch where it's going. So you practice every single day. You stop reacting and start predicting. Your mate throws a ball. Your hand is already moving before it leaves his fingers. Caught it. But where it gets really crazy is when you test it. Tennis. A pro serve can fly at over 250 kilometers an hour. It reaches you in about half a second. Even the pros are half guessing and you can't react at all. So you don't. You watch the toss, the shoulders, the feet. And before the racket even touches the ball, you've decided. Left. The ball goes left. You're already there. The crowd goes silent. Then mental. Then there's your driving test. At 70 miles an hour, a normal driver travels about 21 meters before their foot even reaches the brake. You? 210 meters. That's two football pitches. A cat runs out. You see it straight away. You think break straight away. And then you keep driving. The examiner grabs the handbrake. You fail. You're not allowed to book another one. Then comes bonfire night. A rocket tips over and fires sideways into the crowd. Everyone around you flinches in about a 20th of a second. That flinch is a reflex. Nobody chooses it. Half of them flinch straight into its path. Except you. You've got time. You watch the rocket. You choose one step. Left. It whistles past your ear and into the river. Someone was filming. 30 million views. People start calling you the statue. Scientists want to test you. They try the ruler drop. They hold a ruler above your open hand and let go. Most people catch it after about 20 centimeters. For you to catch it, the ruler would have to fall about 30 meters. That's 10 stories. So they try again. This time you predict. Your hand closes just as they let go. Caught. They write that down, then spend an hour arguing about whether it counts. The DVLA writes to you, "No driving, ever." Your football team politely asks you to stop coming. And then, one morning, there's a letter from the army. Bomb disposal. Because in bomb disposal, the most dangerous thing isn't the bomb, it's a flinch. And there's a cost. People go in for a high five, you leave them hanging. Then, much later, your hand goes up to nobody. Your laugh always arrives late. People think you don't get their jokes. So, you start laughing early, sometimes at the wrong moment. You pick up a hot pan. You feel it straight away. You know to let go straight away. And your hand just keeps holding it. And the worst one. Your little brother trips at the top of the stairs. You see it. You know exactly what to do. And you stand there and watch him fall. He's fine. A bruise. He doesn't even remember. You do. Four years later, the middle of a city, a building site. Behind you, the streets are empty. 10,000 people have been moved out of their homes. In the mud in front of you is a bomb. 500 kilos. Dropped in the Second World War. It's been lying here for over 80 years. Your sergeant is at your shoulder. Steady hands. Slow breathing. The fuse is old, rusted. You're easing it out, millimeter by millimeter. Then, the side of the trench gives way. A slab of wet clay slams into your back. Anyone else would jolt forward. Every muscle at once. A reflex. You don't move. You can't. Your hands stay perfectly still. And then you hear it. Click. Then, ticking. You know this fuse. There's a spring inside. When it lets go, a pin snaps forward. And that's it. Your sergeant shouts, "Run!" Run? Your legs won't even start for ages. You'd still be inside the blast. Pull the fuse out? It's rusted solid. It won't come in time. There's one gap between the pin and the charge, about the width of a thumb. If your thumb is there when the pin fires, it hits you instead. If your thumb gets there late, nothing matters anymore. You can't wait and see. You never could. Whatever you choose, you have to choose it now. Don't watch where it is. Watch where it's going. Ticking. You decide. The longest wait of your life. Ticking. Your hand starts to move. Silence. No ticking, no birds, nothing. You open your eyes. Your thumb is jammed inside the fuse. The pin is resting against it, bent. Your thumb is broken. You don't care. The bomb team's camera caught all of it. Your thumb got there 4/10 of a second before the pin fired. If you'd reacted, you'd have been too late. You didn't react. You were early. That night, 10,000 people go back to their homes. None of them know your name. Your sergeant sits next to you on the back of the truck, says nothing for a long time. "22 years I've done this job. Everybody flinches. Everybody. You're the first one I've ever met who got there before the bomb did. When you get home, someone's waiting outside your door." A man in a gray scarf. You spot him straight away. Your body still hasn't turned round. "2 and 1/2 seconds," he says. "Exactly." He hands you a card. No name, just a time. Tomorrow, 6:00 a.m. There's a door. It opens for 2 and 1/2 seconds. Anyone who moves too soon doesn't come back out. We think you're the only person alive who won't." So, would you want a reaction time 10 times slower?
