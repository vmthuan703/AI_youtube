# What If You Could See 10 Seconds Into the Future?

A glass slides off the table and you catch it before it even starts to fall. A football flies at your head. You've already stepped aside. Your phone buzzes. You say, "Hi, Mom." before it rings. Your mate just stares at you. How did you do that? Easy. You saw it happen 10 seconds ago. Normal humans don't live in the present. They live slightly in the past. Your brain takes about a tenth of a second to make sense of what your eyes see. So, everything you're looking at right now has already happened. But, not for you. You're not behind. You're ahead. So, what if you could see 10 seconds into the future? Here's how it works. Your brain is already a prediction machine. Every step you take, it's guessing where the floor will be. Yours just guesses further. Much further. A normal person reacts in about a quarter of a second. You get 10 seconds of warning. That's 40 times more time than anyone else. Every single moment. It plays like a ghost over your normal sight. A faint second you, one step ahead. But, there's a catch. Two, actually. You only see what your own eyes will see. If you're looking the wrong way in 10 seconds, you miss it. And the future isn't fixed. Every time you change your mind, the vision changes with it. And honestly, it's brilliant. You never miss a bus. You're at the stop at the bottom of the hill before it turns the corner. You catch your keys, your toast, your little sister's ice cream. You win every game of rock, paper, scissors. Every single one. Your friends stop playing. Quiz night? You hear the question before the host reads it. You know which queue at the supermarket will move fastest. You walk through a packed station at rush hour without touching a single person. Like the whole crowd was rehearsed. A pigeon goes for your chips. Not today, pigeon. A girl in a yellow scarf drops her phone at the crossing. You catch it before it hits the ground. She looks at you like you're a wizard. You're not a wizard, you're just early. But where it gets really crazy is sport. A penalty kick reaches the goal in about half a second. Most keepers have to guess. They save roughly one in five. You don't guess. You watch the ball go in 10 seconds before it's kicked. Then you just stand there. Five penalties, five saves. The striker sits down on the grass. So you try sprinting. At the Olympics, if you move less than a tenth of a second after the gun, it's a false start. Your reaction time minus 10 seconds. Disqualified every time. Then a friend takes you to a casino. Roulette. 37 numbers. Bet on one and win. And it pays 35 to one. The ball's still spinning. You can see where it lands. You put 10 pounds on 17. 17. 350 pounds. You do it again and again. By the fourth win, two men in suits are walking towards you. Casinos can turn away anyone they like. And every casino in the country now has your photo. Scientists want to look. They put you in a room with a screen that shows a random number. You call everyone. 100 out of 100. Then 1,000 out of 1,000. One of them drops his clipboard. You catch it. You didn't even look. The videos go everywhere. People start calling you 10. Strangers stop you in the street. Go on. What happens next? You tell them. Then it happens. They scream. Football clubs call. Then the football authorities call. New rule. Nobody who can see the future plays in goal. Your mom frames the letter. And there's a cost. Conversations get strange. You hear what people are going to say, then you hear them say it. You start answering early. People stop finishing their sentences around you. Jokes are worse. You hear every punchline twice. Films, surprise parties, presents, nothing surprises you anymore. And bad news arrives twice. One night, your phone lights up. 10 seconds before it rings, you already know who it is and why. And there are the headaches. Two lots of sight, all day, every day. Some nights, you sit in a dark room just to see one thing at a time. Your best friend says it quietly, "Talking to you is like talking to an echo." You're always 10 seconds ahead, which means you're never quite here. Three years later, you're on the bus home, front seat, top of the hill. The same hill with the bus stop at the bottom. It's half past three. School's out. The crossing at the bottom is packed. You glance at the driver and the ghost in your eyes shows you something new. In 10 seconds, the driver slumps over the wheel. The bus rolls down the hill, 18 tons, faster and faster, straight into the crossing. You look again. The driver's fine. He's humming. He hasn't got a clue. 10 seconds. You try shouting. In the vision, people scream and the bus still hits the crossing. You try running for the door, too slow, same ending. You try grabbing the wheel and pulling left, into the shop fronts, into the people on the pavement. So, you try right, into the stone wall of the bridge. Your side of the bus. The crossing is clear. The girl in the yellow scarf is still there, laughing with her friends. Everyone lives. And then, the vision goes black. It's never gone black before. You only see what your own eyes will see. Six seconds. Nobody else on this bus knows. Nobody else can know. Four. You stand up. Two. You grab the wheel. Silence. Then ringing. Then a voice. You open your eyes. Hospital. Everything hurts. But you're here. The driver had a heart attack at the wheel. He's alive, two rooms down. 31 people were on that crossing. Not one of them was hurt. A paramedic comes in to check on you. She sits on the edge of the bed for a moment. Most people freeze. You moved before anyone knew there was anything to move for. You don't tell her it went black. You don't tell anyone. That night, there's a knock at your door. A woman in glasses. You've never seen her before. And for the first time in years, you didn't see her coming. She smiles. You see 10 seconds. That's cute.
