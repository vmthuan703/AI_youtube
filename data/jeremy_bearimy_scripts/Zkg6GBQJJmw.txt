He looks at the pile. He looks at you. He looks at your dinosaur pajamas. He hangs up and goes back inside. The kid holds out a hand. Can I have my Frisbee? So, what if you could respond? Here's how it works. A normal human gets one life, one. No extra lives, no save points, no try again. The average American lives about 79 years. Your heart beats about 3 billion times, and when it stops for good, that's it, game over. Not you. You're made of about 7 octillion atoms. That's a seven with 27 zeros after it. Arranged in exactly the right way, they make you. Your memories live in the links between about 86 billion brain cells, around 100 trillion connections. Every inside joke, every embarrassing thing you said in fifth grade. And it's fragile. Your brain can only go about 4 to 6 minutes without oxygen before those cells start to die. But not yours. The moment you die, your body vanishes, every atom. 3 seconds later, a perfect copy appears at your respawn point. Healthy, same age, every connection in your brain copied. You remember everything, including how you died. But here's the rule. It only brings back you, not your phone, not your keys, not your clothes. Whatever you're wearing drops right where you died, and you come back in whatever you slept in last night. And it always brings you back to the same spot. Not your bed, the spot where your bed was the day it started, in your mom's house, every time. It never switches off. There's no limit, no pause, no choosing. You don't decide when it happens. Dying does, and only dying resets you. Break your arm? Still broken. Catch a cold? Still sniffling. And honestly, at first, it's the best thing ever. Roller coasters? Boring. Front seat? Hands up? Yawning. The scariest water slide in the park? Head first, backwards, 11 times. A cat is stuck at the top of the tallest tree on the street. You're up there before anyone can say, "Be careful." Your best friend dares you to do anything. Anything. You win every dare, forever. You even eat the gas station sushi. Okay, that one you regret. Food poisoning doesn't kill you, so it doesn't reset. Three days on the bathroom floor, and your mom starts a tally on the fridge. One line for every respawn. She stops saying, "Be careful." She starts saying, "Wipe your feet when you respawn." But, where it gets really crazy is the stuff nobody else can ever do. Take the ocean. The deepest anyone has ever dived on a single breath is about 700 ft. And going down is the easy part. Coming back up is what kills people. You don't have to come back up. 300 ft. The light turns deep blue. 600, it's dark. 1,000, something huge drifts past. An eye the size of a dinner plate. A giant squid. Hardly anyone has ever seen one alive. You've just seen one with your own eyes. Then, nothing. 3 seconds and you're home, dry, in pajamas, on the carpet. Your mom moved the bed to vacuum. Your swim fins? Somewhere at the bottom of the Pacific. And it gets crazier. Rip currents. They can drag a swimmer out to sea at 8 ft a second, faster than an Olympic swimmer. They cause more than eight out of 10 lifeguard rescues on American beaches. A kid is screaming way out past the waves. You're in the water before the lifeguard is off the chair. You reach the kid. You don't fight the current. You swim sideways out of it, back to shore. The lifeguard stares at you. You know you could have died out there? Yeah, that's kind of my thing. And it gets crazier and darker. Carbon monoxide. No color, no smell, no taste. It kills more than 400 Americans every year. Many of them in their sleep. One night, the furnace cracks. The gas creeps into your room. You don't wake up. 3 seconds later, you wake up. Same spot, same room, same gas. And you die again, and again, and again. Gamers have a name for this, a spawn trap. Your mom's detector goes off down the hall. She throws open every window. By then, you've died 19 times. 19 in one night. You slept through every single one. And it gets crazier. Your family goes to Hawaii, 2,500 mi from home. Day two, a beach buffet. You choke on a shrimp. About 5,000 Americans die from choking every year. You're one of them, sort of. 3 seconds later, you're in California, in your pajamas, alone. Your family is in Hawaii. Your phone is in Hawaii. Your wallet is in Hawaii. The waiter is still doing the Heimlich on your shirt. And it gets crazier. Dying resets you. Almost dying doesn't. You land wrong off a skate ramp. You don't die. You break your arm in two places. No reset. About 18,000 Americans get a spinal cord injury every year. Most of them survive. They don't get a do-over. For the first time in a year, you're scared. Not of dying, of not dying. Then the video gets out. The kid next door was filming the whole time. 60 million views in a week. The internet calls you player one. Scientists want to test you. They can't. Killing you is still against the law, even if you come back. Under a US law from 1981, death means your brain stops working for good. Yours never does. So legally, you've never died. Theme parks ban you. Your school bans you from the swim team. Life insurance companies love you. You'll never cash in. The 911 dispatcher knows your address by heart. Is it the respawn kid? Okay, cancel the ambulance. Funny, until the day it's real. And a space agency calls. Mars. About 140 million miles away. Seven months to get there. The hardest part of any Mars mission is getting home. For you, home is three seconds away. Probably. Nobody knows if it works from another planet. And there's a cost. Every death hurts, right up until the end. Your body doesn't know you're coming back. Your nerves still scream. Your heart still pounds. And then it's over. And you remember every second. Your brain is copied perfectly, including the fear. Some nights, you wake up with your hands on your chest, checking.
