# What IF You Could Learn Anything Instantly?

You watch the clip one time, 10 seconds, a guy landing a kickflip. You put your phone away. The whole skate park is watching. The kid who runs this place just landed three in a row. Now he's holding out his board, laughing. 20 bucks says you can't even stand on it. You've never touched a skateboard in your life. You step on, push, pop. The board spins under you, and you land it clean. You flick the board up into your hand. His phone slips out of his. You can learn anything instantly. See it once, read it once, hear it once, and you know it forever. Keep going, and you can speak 40 languages, beat a grandmaster without looking at the board, pull someone out of an avalanche like you've done it a hundred times. And that's when everyone wants what's in your head. But right now, you know exactly one trick. So you do it again, and again. 20 kickflips in a row. He pays you in crumpled ones. And the next morning, you can barely walk down the stairs. See, when a normal person learns a kickflip, they try and fall, and try again, hundreds of times. Every try, the brain cells that control that move connect a little stronger, like a path through tall grass. Walk it enough, and it turns into a road. That's why most skaters need weeks to land their first one, and why people say it takes 10,000 hours to be world-class. That's 3 hours a day for 9 years. You? You skip the walking. One look, and the road is already there, and it stays. A normal person forgets about half of what they learn in a day. You forget nothing. There's just one catch. It only works on your brain. Your muscles, your lungs, your height, all normal. You know how to do anything. Your body still has to keep up. At first, it's the best thing ever. Math test tomorrow? You read the chapter once, 100. Every test, all year. Your teacher checks your desk for notes, twice. Your class bets you can't solve a Rubik's Cube. There are 43 quintillion ways to scramble one. You watch one tutorial, then you solve it in 15 seconds. Most people never solve one at all. 30 people, five bucks each. That's $150. A party. Somebody plays a song with a crazy guitar solo. You've never held a guitar. You listen once, then you borrow his. Note for note. Someone turns the music off just to hear you. Again! You play it again, faster. Game night. Your friends play poker for snacks. You read one book on the odds. Now you know your chances on every hand. About one in three to hit a flush draw by the river. You fold the bad ones, you bet big on the good ones. By midnight, every bag of chips in the house is on your side of the table. Then, video games. You watch a pro play one match. Every route, every trick, every timing. Your fingers are no faster than anyone else's, but you never make a wrong choice. One week later, you're in the top 1% of the whole ranked ladder. Then, basketball tryouts. You watch an hour of the best shooter alive. His feet, his elbow, his release. Pro players make about 78% of their free throws. You make 50 in a row. The coach puts you on the team on the spot. First game, you go up for a layup. A guy who's 6'4 swats it into the bleachers. Because you know everything a pro knows, but you're 5'7. And by the third quarter, your legs are gone. So, you lift. And here's the wild part. When a normal person starts lifting, most of the strength they gain in the first few weeks isn't new muscle. It's their brain learning how to use the muscle they already have. You skip that part. Day one, you lift like you've trained for a month. After that, normal speed. Real muscle still takes months. Playoffs. Two seconds left, down by two. The 6'4 guy is on you again. You can't jump over him. So, you don't. You fake. He jumps, you step back. Three, swish, buzzer. Then, a racetrack. Your friend's uncle lets you watch one on-board video. One lap. Every breaking point, every line. You climb into a race car for the first time in your life. Lap three, you're 2 seconds faster than the uncle. He's raced for 20 years, but in the fast corners, the car pulls your head sideways. It feels twice as heavy. Pro drivers train their necks for years. Yours is a normal neck. By lap 10, you can't hold your head up. You pull into the pits and lie down on the concrete. Still the fastest time of the day. Then, you get bored. So, you read chess books. 12 of them in one weekend. Every opening, every trap, every famous game ever played. Sunday, the park. The old guys who play for five bucks a game. They take money from strangers all day. You win eight in a row. By the next weekend, they start packing up when they see you coming. So, you go bigger. There are only about 2,000 grandmasters on Earth. Most of them spent 15 years getting there. You challenge one. He's played since he was five. Online, live. 300,000 people watching. And you play blindfolded. Move 40, he resigns. The chat explodes. Then, a trip to Japan. For an English speaker, Japanese is one of the hardest languages there is. About 2,200 hours of class to get good. You read a grammar book and a dictionary on the flight. 13 hours. You land fluent. Two guys on the train are laughing about your hoodie. You lean in. In perfect Japanese, "It's light blue. Thanks for noticing." They get off at the next stop. The old lady next to you starts clapping. Then, Spanish, French, Korean, Arabic. There are about 7,000 languages on Earth. By the end of summer, you speak 40. The world goes nuts. Your videos hit 100 million views. Universities send letters. A tech company offers you a million dollars a year just to read their manuals. Scientists put you in a brain scanner. You learn how the scanner works while you're lying in it. Trivia nights ban you. Your teachers ask you to stop correcting them. Every pro team wants a meeting. Then a coach calls, the national snowboard team. Ever been on a snowboard? Never, not once. Day one, you watch a single run by an Olympic rider. Then you drop into a black diamond. You hit the biggest jump on the mountain, backflip, clean landing. Day two, you try a double flip off the same jump. Your brain knows it perfectly. Your body has never done anything like it. You land short, your wrist snaps, six weeks in a cast. Knowing how to do something doesn't make your bones any stronger. On the chairlift, you read the avalanche safety course, 20 minutes, just in case. And there's a cost. Your best friend has practiced guitar for two years. He plays you the song he's been working on, the one from the party. You learned it in three minutes. He doesn't play much after that. Nothing feels like winning anymore. No struggle, no first time. Every trophy feels like a cheat code, and some people think you cheated. And you can't forget anything. A normal memory lets the bad stuff fade. Yours doesn't. Every mean thing anyone ever said to you, word for word, every night. Only about 60 people on Earth are known to remember their lives like this. Most of them say it isn't a gift. Two years later, a backcountry trip with your best friend. 3 ft of fresh snow in 2 days. You read the snow as you ride, the layers, the cracks, the weak layer buried underneath. This slope is wrong. We need to go back, now. Your friend laughs. You read one book. Then you hear it, a crack, like a gunshot. The whole mountain moves. An avalanche can hit 80 miles an hour. You cut left, your friend doesn't. 1 second he's there, the next he's gone, under a river of white. Then silence. Get someone out in 15 minutes and they almost always live. By 35 minutes, most don't. No cell signal. The nearest rescue team is 40 minutes away. Your friend has 15. Your beacon picks up his signal. You learn this from one book, on a chairlift, 60 ft, 20, four. You push the probe into the snow. It hits something soft, 3 ft down. Snow from an avalanche sets almost like concrete. You know the fastest way to dig. Not straight down, from the side, downhill. And there's only you. Under the snow, his air is running out. Then you hear it again, another crack, higher up. You read the slope above you. You know exactly what's coming. The rest of the hill, in about 2 minutes. Run now and you live. Keep digging and you could both be buried. Nobody else even knows it's coming. Only you. But you know one more thing. Where it'll run, left of the big rock. You're 12 ft to the right. Probably. You keep digging. Your arms are screaming. Your brain knows the perfect way to dig. Your body is just a normal body. 13 minutes. Your shovel hits his jacket. You clear his face. He gasps. The roar comes. The snow thunders past. 12 ft away. Then, it stops. The rescue team flies in. Their guide has 20 years in the mountains. Where did you learn to do that? A book. On a chairlift. Your best friend sits wrapped in a blanket, shaking. Next time you say we need to go back, we go back. The next morning, you can't lift your arms. Just like the skate park. Totally worth it. A week later, a black car pulls up outside. A woman in a gray suit hands you a folder. Pages of symbols. Nothing like any of the 40 languages you know. But they repeat, like words. Two weeks ago, this came from space. Nobody on Earth can read it. And you think I can? We think you're the only one who can learn it. You open the folder. So, would you want to learn anything instantly? Tell us in the comments.
