So, you hire a driver. Beep. Approved. The guy at the register stares at the receipt, then at you. Who are you? Honestly, I'm still working that out. A normal 15-year-old has to earn money slowly. Mowing lawns, babysitting, bagging groceries. Even when you grow up, a typical full-time worker in America earns about $60,000 a year. Rent, food, bills, car payments, and whatever's left over, maybe a vacation. The odds of winning the Powerball jackpot? About 1 in 292 million. And even that runs out. Yours doesn't. Ever. So, what if you had infinite money? Here's how it works. You've got one black card. No name, no limit, no bank can explain it. And there's only one. Lose it, and it's gone. When you pay, the money just appears in the store's account, brand new. Nobody loses a cent. So, free money. What's the catch? Well, what actually is money? In America, there's about 22 trillion dollars of it. And almost all of that isn't coins or bills. It's just numbers on screens. Money is a promise. A way of saying, "I'll trade you this for that." But money isn't stuff. Printing more money doesn't make more bread. Picture an island. 100 loaves of bread. 100 dollars on the whole island. One loaf costs about a dollar. Now, double the money. Still 100 loaves. So, now each loaf costs two dollars. That's inflation. Too much money chasing the same stuff. The Federal Reserve tries to keep prices rising about 2% a year. Slow, steady, boring. Just how they like it. So, here's the rule. Your card never runs out, but every dollar it makes is brand new. Spend a little and nobody notices. Spend a lot and every price in the country goes up for everyone. Your money is infinite. The world's stuff isn't. But honestly, the first month is amazing. You never look at a price tag again, not once. You pay off your mom's mortgage. She cries. Then she checks the bank app. Then she cries again. You turn your bedroom into a gaming room. Every console, every game, six screens, a chair that costs more than most cars. You buy courtside seats for the whole season. Then you buy the seats next to them for your friends. You buy pizza for your entire school. Every student, every teacher, even the vice principal who gave you detention. Your best friend still buys the pizza on Fridays, every time. My turn. It's always his turn. He won't let you pay. Not once. You fly first class. Then you buy the plane. Your mom's old car breaks down. You buy her a new one and one for the neighbor and one for the neighbor's dog, just in case. And every bill that comes in the mail, paid before you even open it. But where it gets really crazy is when you think bigger. You want a Ferrari? Easy. You want every new Ferrari? Not so easy. They only make about 14,000 a year. Infinite money and you're still on a waiting list. Money can buy things. It can't make them faster. And it gets crazier. Sports. You want your own football team, a pro one. The most valuable team in the league is worth about 13 billion dollars. You could buy it before breakfast, but you can't just buy it. The other owners have to vote. You need 24 of the 32 to say yes. They take one look at a 15-year-old with a black card and they say no. And it gets crazier. Space. A trip to the space station costs tens of millions of dollars per seat. You buy every seat on the rocket. You take your mom and your best friend. Your mom floats past the window eating a cookie. Don't you dare get crumbs in the air vents. And it gets crazier. Gold. All the gold ever mined would fit in a cube about 72 feet wide, about as tall as a seven-story building. At today's prices, it's worth tens of trillions of dollars. So, you start buying. And every time you buy, the price goes up. The more you want something, the more it costs, even for you. And it gets crazier. Counting. Your balance doesn't have a number, just a little sideways eight. If you counted $1 every second, a million would take you 11 days. A billion? 32 years. A trillion? 32,000 years. You'd have had to start long before anyone had built a city. And it gets crazier. Houses. You buy every house on your street. You give them to the people living in them. Keys for everyone. A block party. Balloons, cake. And then, something strange. The houses on the next street go up in price. 20% in a month. Huh. Then the world notices. The bank tests the card. Every chip, every layer. It's just plastic. A normal card. Except, it isn't. The government loves you. At first, most things you buy come with sales tax, around 7%. So, every time you spend a billion dollars, about 70 million of it goes to the government. You're the biggest taxpayer in history. And you've never had a job. Charities line up around the block. One study says it would cost about $33 billion a year extra to end most of the world's hunger by 2030. You pay it. Day one. But money can't build a road through a war. It can't make a harvest grow faster. Some of it helps. A lot of it gets stuck. Then, the fame. 100 million followers. Your face on a mural. People call you the bottomless. You get 4,000 texts a day. Two of them are from people you actually know. Then, the rules. The Federal Reserve invites you in. Cookies. Very small cups of coffee. Your spending is now bigger than some countries. Please, slow down. Congress argues for 11 hours about a law to stop you. Some senators want a limit. Others want you to pay for a new hospital first. They're still arguing. Some countries stopped taking your dollars altogether. Thank you. We're fine. And there's a cost. At first, it's small. You buy your mom a birthday present. Then you realize you can buy anything. So, nothing means anything. She smiles at the diamond necklace. She cried at the macaroni card you made her when you were six. Your phone never stops. Everyone wants something. Old friends, new friends, kids at school who never talked to you before. Your best friend gets quiet. Still buys the pizza. But, the pizza goes cold. It's not that I'm jealous. It's just I don't know what I can give you anymore. And then, the quietest one. You're at the corner store. Your mom's counting coins for bread. A loaf used to cost about $2. Now it's three. She doesn't say anything. She doesn't have to. You did that. One year later. Prices are rising faster than they have since 1980, when US inflation hit almost 15%. Savings are melting. Retirement money is shrinking. Every news channel has your face on it. So, you decide to fix everything. All at once. The country owes almost 40 trillion dollars. The national debt. You pay it off. Every cent. One afternoon. For a moment, the whole country cheers. Then, the money lands. Almost 40 trillion brand new dollars. That's almost twice all the money in America in one afternoon. And people panic. Not about now, about next. If you can do that once, you can do it again and again. Around the world, everyone starts selling dollars. The dollar falls. 5%, 10, 15. On the corner store window, the price of bread gets crossed out, then crossed out again. In 2008, Zimbabwe printed so much money, prices were doubling about every 25 hours.
