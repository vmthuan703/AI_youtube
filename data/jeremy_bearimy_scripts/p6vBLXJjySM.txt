# What If You Had Earthbending?
video_id: p6vBLXJjySM | đăng: 2026-11-04 | lượt xem: 33725 | outlier: 0.35

You stomp your foot and your best friend's house moves. The porch creaks, the windows rattle, the whole house shutters across the lawn. 10 of your friends are filming. The mailman drops the mail. 50 bucks says you can't move it off its spot. That was the bet. You have earthbending. Dirt, sand, rock, stone. If it came out of the ground, it moves when you move. And every day you train, it gets stronger. Strong enough, one day, to move a whole neighborhood out of the way of the ocean. And that's when it stops being a game. But right now, you move the house 1 in. 1 in. Then your legs give out and you sit down in the grass. Your friend looks at the house, looks at you. Pay up. Then the front door bangs open. His mom, soaking wet, holding a dish towel. Who broke the kitchen? See, a house doesn't just sit on the ground. It's plugged into it. Water pipes, sewer pipes. Move it 1 in and everything under the sink snaps. A normal person can lift about 100 lb. A shovel of dirt weighs about 15. A chunk of rock the size of a microwave weighs as much as a grown man. And a house? About 80 tons, as much as 40 cars. Earthbending works like a muscle you never knew you had. Right now, it's about as strong as your arms. You can lift one big rock, push a little dirt. Metal doesn't listen. Not yet. The house only moved because you shoved the ground under it with everything you had. Like a regular guy pushing a stuck car. It moves, barely. But muscles grow. Train every day and your strength doubles every month. 100 lb, then 200, then 400. Double it 12 times and by the end of the year, you can move about 200 tons. Two houses. So you train. Month one, 200 lb, mini golf. You don't aim, you tilt the green. The ball rolls uphill, around the windmill, and drops in. 18 holes, 18 shots. That's cheating. Blame the green. Your best friend bets you 20 bucks you can't break the stone skipping world record. 88 skips. You flick the stone. It skips, and skips, and skips. Every time it touches the lake, you push it back up. 89. Then it turns around and skips straight back into his lap. Double or nothing? Concrete is just crushed rock glued together. So, it listens, too. Math class. You raise the floor under your friend's desk 1 in a minute. He doesn't notice. 40 minutes later, he's taking notes with his chin. Why is everybody laughing? Month three, 800 lb. Your friends want a pool. A digger takes 2 days to dig one. You take one afternoon. You lift the dirt out in chunks. Stack it into a wall. Then, a diving board made of stone. One problem. Dig past a few feet and you can hit ground water. Water that's been sitting under your yard the whole time. The pool fills by itself in 10 minutes. It's brown. Your friend jumps in anyway. Month five, a ton and a half. Track day, the 100 m. Most kids your age run it in about 14 seconds. The world record is 9.58. You don't run. You surf. The ground rolls under your feet like a wave, 40 mph. You cross the line in 6 seconds. Arms crossed, not even breathing hard. The coach checks his stopwatch, then the rule book. Your feet have to do the running. Disqualified. Month six, 3 tons. Baseball. You're in center field, bottom of the ninth. The ball is crushed. It's flying toward the fence, 400 ft. You stop. A pillar of dirt shoots up under your feet, 12 ft high. You reach over the wall and catch it. The whole crowd goes silent. Then, it explodes. The pillar sinks back down. You toss the ball to the umpire. The batter is still standing at home plate, holding his helmet. Month seven, six tons. Your best friend's dirt bike. A normal backyard jump is about 20 ft. He hits the ramp and you keep building it under his wheels, higher. He lands 60 ft away, screaming. Happy screaming. Then, he lands in the neighbor's hedge, less happy. But, where it gets crazy is when you find out you're not the only one. Month eight, 13 tons. A video goes viral. Some guy, two states away, red hoodie. He rips a boulder out of a hill and throws it into a lake. The caption says, "Second best? Prove it." He's been training longer, two years. He's stronger than you, by a lot. Midday, an old quarry. 2,000 people watching the live stream. He doesn't say hi, he just stomps. A slab of rock the size of a fridge flies at you, about three tons, 60 mph. You punch. It splits in half and flies past your ears. Then, the ground grabs your feet. He's sinking you, ankles, knees. You're stuck. You stomp, hard. The rock under you kicks up like a trampoline and launches you out. He throws another, bigger. You duck. It smashes the quarry office into splinters. He's strong, but every throw is huge, slow, loud. You can feel his next move coming through the ground before he makes it. So, you go small. You drop the ground under his front foot, 2 in, that's all. He stumbles. You raise a ridge behind his heel. He falls flat on his back. Then, you close the ground around him, up to his chest, snug, like a sleeping bag made of rock. He tries to push. Nothing moves. "Okay. Okay. You win." 2,000 people lose it in the chat. Then, you both start coughing. Crushing rock makes dust. Superfine. Sharp. Breathe in enough of it for long enough and it scars your lungs for good. That's why quarry workers wear masks. You both go home with a sore throat and a rematch. The fight hits 80 million views by morning. And the world goes nuts. A construction company offers you $10,000 a day to dig basements. The city asks you to fix every pothole. Your school asks you to stop redecorating the parking lot. Every kid on your street wants a pool. So does every dad. Most of the dads. Scientists put a sensor in your backyard. Every time you stomp, it shows up like a tiny earthquake. A magnitude one. The same as a truck rumbling by. But it's coming from one kid in a hoodie. And there's a cost. On airplanes, you go blind. No ground. Nothing to feel. For 3 hours, you grip the armrest like you're falling. And there's always sand. In your shoes. In your bed. In your cereal. And your friends stop betting. Not because you win. Because you never lose. And that's no fun for anybody. 1 year later. 200 tons. A hurricane is heading for the coast. And the beach town where your grandma lives sits right on the dunes. Storm waves don't just flood houses. They eat the sand under them. One big storm can rip away 50 ft of beach in a single night. Houses here have already fallen into the ocean. Whole. Like toys off a shelf. People have moved buildings away from the sea before. Engineers once slid a whole lighthouse almost 3,000 ft inland. On rails. It took 23 days. Normally, moving one house takes a crew about 2 weeks and about $100,000. You've got one night. You plant your feet. Breathe. And the sand under the first house turns to stone. One giant flat rock, like a tray. Then you pull. The whole house slides. Slow, steady, 200 ft back, away from the water. Porch light still on. Your grandma's house goes first. She rides it the whole way, standing on the porch, holding her coffee. Then the next house, and the next. And the guy pulling the house next to yours, red hoodie. Don't get excited. I just wanted to see who moves more. 11 houses, midnight. Your legs are shaking. Your nose is bleeding. Then the last one. Your grandma's neighbor. A little kid's face in the upstairs window, too scared to come out. A wave hits, 12 ft tall. The dune under the house collapses. The house tips toward the water. And at the same second, the sand under your rival gives way, too. He goes in. The water drags him out, fast. You've got enough left for one more push. One. The house or him? Grab the house and he's alone out there. Grab him and the house goes in, with the kid. He comes up, coughing, choking on seawater. The house! Get the house! You grab the house. Stone shoots up under it. You drag it, 10 ft, 20, 50, up onto solid ground. The kid is safe. You turn around. The water is empty. One second, two, three, then a fist of rock punches up out of the waves. A pillar. And on top of it, red hoodie, soaked, coughing, alive. He found the bottom and made a pillar, just like yours at the ball game. He watched the video. By sunrise, the storm is gone. 12 houses stand 200 ft back from where they used to be. Not one fell in. An old house mover walks down the beach, 40 years on the job. He looks at the houses, then at you. Best I ever did was one house a week. You did 12 in one night. Your grandma brings you a coffee. Her porch now looks at the parking lot instead of the ocean. The view was better yesterday. Grandma, kidding. Thank you. Your rival sits down next to you, still dripping. You picked right. I know. Still want that rematch? Anytime. Back home, your best friend hands you a crumpled $50 bill. Fine, you moved a house. That night, there's a knock at the door. A stranger, gray coat, calm, too calm. Sand, dirt, rock, that's the easy stuff. She holds out a car key. It bends in her palm, slowly, all by itself. Metal comes out of the ground, too. Every car, every bridge, every skyscraper.
