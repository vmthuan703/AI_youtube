It's the coldest day of the winter, 9° everyone's wearing three jackets. You're wearing a t-shirt. Snowball fight, you against your best friend. He packs one hard, ice in the middle, throws it right at your face. You breathe out, punch. The snowball turns to steam a foot from your nose. A little kid on a sled stares at you, mouth open. Do that again. Throw one. He throws one. Gone. Again. Then cold air goes up your nose. Uh-oh. You sneeze. A fireball the size of a beach ball shoots out of your face, right into the snowman. His head melts. His carrot nose drops into the slush. The kid looks at the snowman, looks at you. I built him. Sorry. It took me all morning. I said sorry. He picks up the carrot, holds it out to you. Can you at least cook this? So, what if you had fire bending? Here's how it works. Your body is already a furnace. Sitting still, it makes about 100 watts of heat, like a bright light bulb that never turns off. Sprinting, it makes 10 times that. Fire needs three things: heat, fuel, oxygen. You're the heat. Your breath is the oxygen. Breathe in and the energy builds in your chest. Breathe out and punch and it leaves your fist as fire. Kick and it comes out of your foot. A slow breath makes a candle. A big one makes a blast. And any fire near you? A candle, a campfire, a stove. You can bend it, grow it, shrink it, snuff it out. But your fire runs on how you feel. Calm makes it small and sharp, exactly where you aim it. Angry makes it big, wild, hungry. And you can't switch that off, ever. When your heart races, your hands get hot. And at first, it's amazing. You're never cold again. You walk to school in shorts in January. Microwave? Never again. You hold the plate. 30 seconds, breakfast is done. One problem. Polyester melts at about 500°. Every time you get annoyed, your hoodie gets a hole in it. You lose four hoodies in one week. Now, you only wear cotton. Then, your friends start betting on you. 20 bucks you can't light the grill from the porch. That's 30 ft. You flick two fingers. The grill lights. 40 bucks you can't light all 16 candles. One breath. Easy. One breath out. 16 little flames pop up one after another. Your best friend hands over the money. "I'm never betting against you again." He bets against you again the next day. Then, you take it to the skate park. You drop in, hit the ramp, and right at the top, you push fire out of both feet. Fire jets. You don't come down. You keep going. 10 ft, 15. You flip, spin, and land on the deck like it's nothing. The whole skate park stops skating. "Pro is a rocket." Basketball. Your normal jump? 16 in. But, you start at the free throw line, 15 ft from the hoop. Two steps, one blast from your heels, and you're flying. Dunk. The rim is 10 ft up. You're looking down at it. The other team calls a timeout. Not to make a plan, just to watch the replay. Then, it's the 4th of July. The whole town is at the stadium. 30,000 people. And the fireworks are soaked. A storm hit the truck. Not one fuse will light. The mayor looks at the crowd, then at you. You walk to the middle of the field. 30,000 phones go up. You breathe in, slow, deep, and you punch the sky. A spiral of fire climbs 200 ft into the dark. Then another. Then a giant eagle made of flame circling the stadium. Then you clap and it bursts into a thousand sparks. The crowd loses it. By morning, the video has 50 million views. Firefighters want to study you. Scientists want to measure you. Your school bans you from the chemistry lab for everyone's safety. One fire captain gives you a tip anyway. Fire's simple, kid. Take away the heat, the fuel, or the air and it dies. And every party, every camp, every bonfire wants you there. Which is how you end up at the end of summer bonfire on the hill behind the old red barn. It hasn't rained in 6 weeks. The grass is dry as paper. You're showing off, spinning a ring of fire over your head. Everyone's cheering. Then the wind picks up. One spark, tiny, it floats off the ring and lands in the grass. You don't even see it. By the time somebody screams, it's not a spark anymore. It's a line of fire racing up the hill. You try to pull it back, but there's too much of it. And now you're scared. And scared is just as bad as angry. The fire gets bigger. A grass fire can move up to 14 miles an hour, faster than you can run uphill. And the wind throws embers ahead of it, hundreds of feet. One lands on the barn roof. The barn is gone in 20 minutes. The whole hillside goes black. Nobody's hurt, but in the morning you stand in the ashes, smoke still curling up, fire trucks still dripping. You didn't mean to. It doesn't matter. Almost nine out of 10 wildfires are started by people. Now, you're one of them. After that, people talk. One guy talks the loudest, red hoodie, your rival since middle school. At the court, he points at you. "Look out, barn burner's here." You ignore him. "Somebody get a hose." You ignore him again. Then he shoves your little brother off the bench. Your brother hits the ground hard, and your heart goes from 70 to 130 in 1 second. You don't decide to do it. Your fist just moves. Fire. It catches his sleeve. He screams. Your best friend tackles him and smothers it with his jacket. It's out in 3 seconds. 3 seconds is enough. At 156° skin burns badly in 1 second. Your fire is over 1,000. Hospital. Second-degree burns all down his arm. His mom won't look at you. You sit in the hallway, hands in your lap. They're still warm. You keep replaying it. The smell, the sound he made. You wanted him to stop talking. You never wanted this. So, you start training. Not your fire, your temper. Here's the science. When you get angry, adrenaline floods your body. Your breathing gets fast and shallow. Your heart pounds. But breathing works both ways. Slow it down and your heart follows. In for 4 seconds, out for 6. 1 minute of that and your heart rate drops. So, every night you sit in your room with one candle. You breathe in, the flame grows. You breathe out, it shrinks. Your job? Keep it the same size for 10 minutes. The first night you last 8 seconds. Then the flame jumps to the ceiling. The 10th night, 1 minute. The 50th night, 10 minutes. The flame doesn't flicker once. And something weird happens. The calmer you get, the stronger your fire gets. Not bigger, sharper, tighter, hotter. At school, a guy trips you in the hallway, on purpose. Your hands heat up. You breathe in, 4. Out, 6. Nothing happens. You just walk away. It's the coolest thing you've ever done. And nobody even saw it. A week later, your neighbor's grill flares up. Grease fire. The flames climb the fence. The old you would have panicked. Now, you just breathe and pull the heat out of it. The fire shrinks to nothing. Your neighbor drops his spatula. But, there's a cost. Your mom keeps a fire extinguisher by your bed, just in case you have a bad dream. When you raise your voice, people step back. Your friends, even your little brother. And you never get to just lose it. No yelling at the TV, no slamming doors, no screaming into a pillow. You feel everything. You just can't let it out. One year later, the playoff game. The gym is packed. Fourth quarter. Then the smell hits you. Smoke. A fire in the storage room. Mats, paint, cleaning supplies. Black smoke pouring under the doors. Everyone runs for the exits, but one door won't open. The locker room. Somebody's still in there. You know that voice. Red hoodie. The firefighters are 4 minutes away. Smoke can fill a room in less than three. And smoke kills more people than flames do. You don't wait. Heat, fuel, air. You remember. You walk into the fire. It's like walking into a hurricane. Heat everywhere. You raise both hands and pull. The flames bend away from you. They curl into a ball over your head. Bigger and bigger. You're holding the whole fire now. Your arms shake. Sweat runs into your eyes. Then you see him, on the floor, coughing. His arm still scarred from what you did. He looks up at you. Why did you come back for me? And for 1 second, the old anger comes back. All of it. The barn, the court, everything he ever said. The fire above you surges. The ceiling starts to crack. Get angry now and it all comes down. On him, on you. You could drop it and run. Save yourself. Or, you could breathe. In, four. Out, six. The fire stops growing. In, four. Out, six. It shrinks. smaller, smaller, until it's a candle flame in your palm. You close your hand. It's gone. Outside, the firefighters stare at the gym. One burned room. Nothing else touched.
