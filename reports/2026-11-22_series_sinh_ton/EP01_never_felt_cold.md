## Script

It's minus fifteen outside. Snow up to your knees. Everyone at the bus stop is wrapped in three layers, stamping their feet, breath like smoke. And you're standing there in a T-shirt. Shorts. No gloves. Totally comfortable.

"Bro," says Leo, your best friend. "Are you insane?"

Here's how it works. You never feel cold. Not a shiver, not a chill, nothing. But that's the only thing that changes. Your body still loses heat like everyone else's. Your skin still freezes. The universe just stopped telling you about it.

You're Sam, fifteen. And in nine days, that difference is going to matter more than anything.

But right now? Right now it's amazing. The first thing you notice is that you're never tired in winter anymore. You don't hunch. You don't rush. While the whole class sprints from the bus to the doors, you stroll. A girl from your science class, Maya, stares at you like you're a ghost.

"How are you not dead?"

You shrug. "Warm-blooded."

It gets better. You win twenty dollars off Leo by sitting in the school's ice bath after football for ten full minutes. The coach uses that tub for exactly ninety seconds at a time. You're scrolling on your phone. The team starts filming. By lunch, the video has four thousand views.

You eat ice cream outside in January. You sleep with the window open. Your mom turns the heating off in your room and says it's the cheapest winter she's ever had.

And you start reading about why this even feels weird. Turns out, your skin is covered in tiny sensors. One of them, a protein called TRPM8, fires whenever something cold touches you. It's the same sensor that makes mint feel cold. In 2021, the scientists who figured out how we sense temperature won the Nobel Prize. Your sensors? Still there. They just never reach the part of your brain that says, "this hurts."

Then Leo's dad invites you both on a winter camping trip. Real one. Tents in the snow, two nights, no cabin.

The first night, it drops to minus twenty. Leo is inside two sleeping bags, wearing a hat, a scarf, and three pairs of socks, and he's still shaking so hard the tent rattles. You're lying on top of your sleeping bag in a hoodie, staring at the stars through the open flap.

"I hate you," Leo whispers.

"You want my bag?"

"I want your brain."

And here's the weird part. Around two in the morning, you notice your own jaw is clicking. Your arms are trembling. You're shivering. You just can't feel why. Shivering isn't something you decide to do. A tiny area deep in your brain, the hypothalamus, watches your core temperature, and when it drops, it makes your muscles shake on their own. Shaking hard can push your body's heat production to several times its normal level. Your body is still fighting the cold. You're just not invited to the meeting.

You don't tell Leo. You zip yourself into the bag. And for the first time since this started, you feel something close to scared.

In the morning it's gone. The sun is out, you're building a snow wall in a T-shirt, and Leo's dad is laughing so hard he has to sit down. You forget about the night.

That's mistake number one.

But where it gets crazy is the lake.

There's a frozen lake behind the school. Every winter, kids dare each other to jump into the hole the fishermen cut in the ice. Nobody lasts more than a few seconds. Normal people, the second they hit water that cold, gasp. They can't help it. It's called cold shock. For about one minute, your breathing goes wild, and if your head is under water when you gasp, that's how people drown.

You jump in. And you don't gasp. You float there, calm, looking up at fifty kids on the ice screaming your name. You climb out and walk home with wet hair.

Leo is walking next to you, but he's quiet.

"What?" you ask.

"Nothing," he says. "It's just… you're kind of a different person now."

And it gets crazier.

A local news channel hears about "the boy who doesn't feel cold." Then a bigger one. A sports drink company emails your mom. They want you to stand outside on a mountain in a T-shirt for an ad. They're offering five thousand dollars.

The shoot is at the top of a ski resort. The wind up there is brutal. The crew are in full snow suits. You're in shorts. Between takes, the director keeps saying, "Incredible. Incredible. One more."

Four hours. You don't even notice the time.

On the drive home, your mom looks down at your hands. And she goes silent.

Your fingertips are white. Not pale. White, like wax. Hard to the touch.

That's frostbite.

At the hospital, the doctor explains it slowly. When it's minus twenty-eight with wind, uncovered skin can start freezing in about thirty minutes. You were out there for four hours. Normal people would have been screaming to go inside after twenty minutes. That pain isn't a weakness. It's an alarm. And you don't have it anymore.

You keep all ten fingers. Barely. But two of them will be numb for months.

And there's a cost.

It's not just the fingers. You've been so busy being "the cold kid" that you've missed things. Leo's birthday. You were at the shoot. You told yourself he'd understand.

He didn't.

He's been sitting with other people at lunch. When you sit down, he stands up.

"You know what's funny?" he says. "You used to be the one who'd come to the lake just to hold my jacket. Now you're the one everyone films."

You want to say something. Nothing comes out.

At home it's the same. Your mom checks your hands every morning now, before school, like you're six again. She bought a thermometer and makes you take your temperature when you come in from outside. She's not proud anymore. She's scared. And every time she looks at your two numb fingers, you can see her thinking the same thing.

What happens next time, when nobody's watching?

That night you lie in bed with the window open, like always. And for the first time you close it. Not because you're cold. Because you don't know if you are.

Then, day nine.

It's the last week before the lake gets closed for the season. The ice is thinner now. There are signs everywhere. Leo is out there anyway, walking across the middle with two boys from the year above. They're laughing. Showing off.

Then a sound. Like a gunshot.

Crack.

Leo is gone.

There's just a black hole in the ice where he was standing. The two other boys run. Not toward him. Away.

You're already moving.

Here's what you know, because you read about it after the lake dare. Rescue experts have a rule for falling into ice water. One. Ten. One. One minute of cold shock, when you gasp and panic. Ten minutes of useful movement before your muscles stop working. And about one hour before you pass out from hypothermia.

Leo is in the first minute. He's gasping, splashing, going under.

You don't have the first minute. You never gasp. So you have something nobody else on this lake has. Time.

You get flat on your stomach to spread your weight, the way the safety signs say. You slide toward the hole. The ice groans under you.

"Leo! Look at me! Breathe! Just breathe!"

He comes up, coughing. His eyes find yours.

Ten minutes. That's what both of you have now.

You reach out. His hand is too far. You slide closer. The ice cracks again. And then you're in the water too.

For everyone else, this is the worst moment. But you don't feel a thing. Your head is clear. And that's dangerous, because water steals your body heat about twenty-five times faster than air does. You can't feel it, but your muscles are already slowing down.

Eight minutes.

You get behind Leo. "Arms on the ice. Kick your legs. Like swimming. Kick!"

He tries. He slides back.

Six minutes.

Your fingers aren't listening to you anymore. You can't feel them anyway, but now they won't close. That's the cold winning. You just didn't get the warning.

Four minutes.

You get your shoulder under him. You push. Everything you have. He kicks. His chest hits the ice, then his waist, then he rolls. Away from the hole. Just like the signs say.

He's out.

You're not.

You try to lift your arms onto the ice. They feel like somebody else's arms. Slow. Heavy. Your body is shutting down and your brain is completely calm about it, and that's the scariest thing you've ever felt.

Two minutes.

Then a hand grabs your hood.

Leo. Flat on his stomach. Shaking so hard his teeth are clicking. Pulling.

"You held my jacket for three years," he says through his teeth. "I'm holding yours."

He pulls. You kick. And you slide out onto the ice.

The ambulance gets there eleven minutes later. Your body temperature is thirty-three degrees. Below thirty-five, doctors call it hypothermia. The nurse tells you that if Leo had waited one more minute, you wouldn't be here.

That night, in the hospital, Leo is in the bed next to yours. Wrapped in a foil blanket. Still shivering.

"Hey," he says. "Does it hurt? Not feeling it?"

You think about it. "I think I'd rather feel it."

And that's when a man in a dark coat walks into the room. He's not a doctor. He sits on the end of your bed like he's done this a hundred times.

"We know what you are," he says. "There are seven others like you. Nobody else can survive where we're going."

He puts a photo on your blanket. White ice. Nothing else. Written on the back: Antarctica. Minus eighty.

"We leave Friday."
